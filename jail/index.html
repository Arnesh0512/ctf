About the Cellular Jail

The Historic Kāla Pānī

Constructed by the British colonial administration between 1896 and 1906 at Atlanta Point in Port Blair, the Cellular Jail was specifically engineered to exile political dissidents and freedom fighters from mainland India across the Bay of Bengal into solitary confinement.

The remote archipelago location and the turbulent ocean gave rise to the historic term Kāla Pānī (Black Waters), symbolizing an exile from which few returned. Notable patriots imprisoned here include Vinayak Damodar Savarkar, Batukeshwar Dutt, Yogendra Shukla, Bhai Parmanand, and hundreds of revolutionaries.

National Memorial Status

Declared a National Memorial in 1979 by the Government of India, the complex stands as a poignant tribute to the resilience, courage, and supreme sacrifices made by India's freedom fighters during the independence struggle.

COLONIAL ARCHITECTURE

Seven-Wing Panopticon Design

Central Watchtower

The fulcrum of the entire complex. A three-storey brick watchtower from which sentries possessed an unobstructed 360-degree radial view across all seven wings.

Solitary Confinement Cells

Each cell measured approximately 13.5 × 7.5 feet with a solitary ventilator at 9.8 feet. Cells in opposing wings faced only blank back walls, strictly precluding any prisoner communication.

The Surviving Wings

While four wings suffered damage during World War II and the 1941 earthquake, three original wings (Wings 1, 6, and 7) remain standing today as preserved heritage galleries.
